# DRB Ignite — Discussion Slide Deck & Facilitation Guide

## Format: Facilitated Discussion (up to 2 hours)
**Attendees**: Directors of School Improvement, Finance, Estates (+ Alvin Walters, IT & Compliance)
**Date**: March 2026

### How to Use This Document
Each "slide" below has:
- **SLIDE CONTENT** — what goes on screen (keep minimal, visual where possible)
- **TALKING POINTS** — what you say while showing the slide
- **DISCUSSION PROMPT** — the question you ask the room. *Let them talk.* Listen. Respond to what they say, not what you planned to say next
- **LISTEN FOR** — signals that tell you what they care about, so you can steer later slides

### Facilitation Principles
- You are *not presenting*. You are leading a conversation about their problems, using your slides as scaffolding
- Spend 30% talking, 70% listening
- Every department director should feel their problems have been heard *before* you offer solutions
- The two advanced sections (Methods Extension, AI Layer) should feel like they *emerged naturally* from the earlier discussion — "you've just described exactly why flat data fails" → transition to structured approach
- If discussion is flowing well, skip slides. The relationship matters more than the deck

## SLIDE 2 — The Problem Statement

**SLIDE CONTENT**:
> **Most data answers "what happened?"**
> **Almost none answers "why?" or "what will happen next?"**
>
> ```
> Dependent variable  →  the OUTCOME  →  what we measure
> Independent variable →  the CAUSE    →  what we should manage
> ```

**TALKING POINTS**:
- "Everything you've just described" [reference their frustrations from Slide 1] "has a common root: we collect the *dependent variable* — the result — when what we need to understand is the *independent variable* — the cause"
- Quick example: "If a child's reading score drops, that's the dependent variable. The *independent* variables are: Were they attending? Were they engaged when present? Did they have the prerequisite skills? Was the teaching method matched to how they learn?"

**DISCUSSION PROMPT**:
> "Can you give me an example from your area where you *know* something is going wrong, but the data only tells you the outcome, not the cause?"

**LISTEN FOR**:
- Specific examples you can refer back to throughout the session

## SLIDE 15 — From Flat Data to Structured Graph

**SLIDE CONTENT**:
> **The Skills Map Approach**
>
> 459 skills. Each has:
> - 5 observable criteria (what does mastery look like?)
> - Parent skills (prerequisites — what must be in place first?)
> - Offspring skills (what this unlocks next)
>
> ```
>  Number Bonds ──→ Place Value ──→ Column Addition
>                                         ↑
>                          Working Memory (prerequisite)
> ```
>
> *"Emma hasn't failed maths. She hasn't consolidated working memory — which means column addition is impossible regardless of how many times she practises it."*

**TALKING POINTS**:
- "Instead of a flat list of scores, I've built a directed graph — a web of dependencies. Every skill has parents (what must already be in place) and offspring (what it enables next)"
- "This means when Emma scores 72% in maths, the system doesn't just flag her as 'below target'. It diagnoses: 'She's mastered number bonds and place value, but column addition requires working memory as a prerequisite, and that isn't consolidated yet. *More maths practice won't help. She needs working memory support*'"
- "That's the difference between a score and a diagnosis"
- **If laptop available**: "Let me show you what this looks like" — open Skills Map

**DISCUSSION PROMPT**:
> "How often do you think interventions are deployed for the wrong level of the problem? Where we're treating the symptom — the maths score — rather than the root cause — the missing prerequisite?"

**LISTEN FOR**:
- Recognition ("all the time") = they're with you
- Scepticism ("teachers know their children") = acknowledge expertise, position the tool as *supporting* teacher judgment with evidence
- Questions about workload = transition to Slide 16

## SLIDE 20 — Privacy by Architecture

**SLIDE CONTENT**:
> **The Federated Approach: Collective Intelligence, Complete Privacy**
>
> 1. Each school runs its own AI instance — self-contained
> 2. AI builds local patterns from student interactions
> 3. **No personally identifiable information ever leaves the school**
> 4. Only *anonymised patterns* are shared centrally
> 5. Improved models pushed back to all schools
>
> *Same architecture Google uses for keyboard prediction on your phone.
> Your typing never leaves the device. The model still improves.*

**TALKING POINTS**:
- "This is the part that addresses every GDPR concern. The architecture is called *federated learning*. It's how Google improves the keyboard on your phone without ever seeing what you type. Your data stays on your device. Only the *patterns* — stripped of all personal information — leave"
- "In our context: each school has its own AI container. It processes data locally. When it detects a pattern — 'students with Profile X tend to disengage in autumn term' — it strips all identifiers, anonymises the pattern, and shares *only the pattern* with the central coordinator"
- "The central coordinator aggregates patterns from all 13 schools and sends improved models back. No school ever sees another school's raw data. No PII ever moves"
- "The ICO wouldn't just approve this — they'd *celebrate* it. Privacy isn't a policy layer. It's the architecture itself"

**DISCUSSION PROMPT**:
> "Does that address the privacy concerns? What would your data protection officer need to see to be comfortable with this?"

**LISTEN FOR**:
- Specific compliance requirements they're working under
- Whether they already have privacy concerns with current systems (Arbor, CPOMS) — this is *better* than what they have
- Alvin may have views on GDPR architecture — let him speak if he wants to

## Reading the Room

| Signal | What It Means | What to Do |
|--------|---------------|------------|
| Director leans forward, asks questions | They're engaged | Let them lead. Answer their questions. Spend more time here |
